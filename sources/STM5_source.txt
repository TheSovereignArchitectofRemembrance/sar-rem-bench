STM-5: The Five-Phase Persistence Law
Statement of the law
STM-5 (Five-Phase Persistence Law):
In any bounded physical system, the conversion of transient throughput (e.g., propagating 
energy/information) into persistent state (stored energy/structure/record) requires a minimum 
sequence of five functional phases: capture, internal redistribution, mode conversion, 
work/selection, and resolution (storage or irreversible dissipation). Any system that reliably 
accumulates persistent state must implement these phases explicitly or implicitly.
Definitions (tight and measurable)
 Throughput: A propagating flux that is not intrinsically persistent within the system 
(e.g., radiation, waves, transient signals, flow).
 Persistent state: A stable configuration whose lifetime is long compared to the 
interaction timescale (e.g., chemical potential, structural order, memory/record, stored 
energy).
 Bounded system: A region or mechanism with definable interactions and constraints 
(physical boundary, potential well, network boundary, control boundary).
 Phase: A functional role in the transformation pipeline (not necessarily a thermodynamic 
phase).
The five phases (canonical form)
Phase 1 – Capture (Containment)
Function: Intercept throughput and prevent immediate escape.
Operational test: If capture is absent, input mostly exits without changing system state beyond 
negligible perturbation.
Examples (physics-safe):
 Absorption cross-section / coupling
 Boundary condition / impedance matching
 Potential well / confinement

Phase 2 – Internal Redistribution (Retention / Echo)
Function: Re-route intercepted energy/information within the system while limiting loss, 
enabling controlled delivery to conversion sites.
Operational test: If redistribution is absent, the system shows high loss to scattering/leakage 
and poor targeting; efficiency collapses under noise.
Examples:
 Diffusive random walk in dense media
 Network routing/relays
 Resonant transfer within a cavity or manifold
Phase 3 – Mode Conversion (Translation)
Function: Convert throughput into a non-propagating or less-escaping form that can support 
persistence and work (e.g., field excitation → potential gradient).
Operational test: If conversion is absent, the system can “light up” transiently but cannot 
accumulate durable capacity.
Examples:
 Radiative → mechanical/thermal → chemical potential
 Signal → state variable
 Wave energy → stored potential
Phase 4 – Work/Selection (Observer / Control Action)
Function: Use converted capacity to drive directed change (bias outcomes) rather than 
symmetric dissipation.
Operational test: If selection is absent, energy thermalizes without producing structured 
outputs; output resembles equilibrium relaxation.
Examples:
 Rectification / gating / thresholding
 Feedback control
 Non-equilibrium biasing of transitions
(Physics-safe note: “observer” here means selection function, not consciousness.)

Phase 5 – Resolution (Storage or Irreversible Dissipation)
Function: Terminate the cycle by committing energy/information to (a) persistent storage or (b) 
irreversible export/dissipation (vent).
Operational test: If resolution is absent, the system oscillates, saturates, or destabilizes; 
persistent state fails to accumulate or decays rapidly.
Examples:
 Chemical storage / structural ordering
 Radiative cooling / exhaust
 Entropy export required for ongoing non-equilibrium operation
Why STM-5 is a law (not just a model)
STM-5 follows from constraints, not domain narratives:
1. You cannot store propagation as propagation in a bounded system without conversion 
(photons/waves don’t “pile up” stably without a container mode).
2. Conversion without capture is impossible (nothing stays long enough to convert).
3. Work without selection collapses into symmetry and dissipation (no net structured 
output).
4. Persistence without resolution fails (no commit step; the system saturates or re￾randomizes).
5. Non-equilibrium persistence requires entropy export (resolution includes venting).
STM-5 is therefore a minimum functional decomposition for persistence under physical 
constraints.
Falsifiability and predictions (this makes it publishable)
STM-5 is falsified if you can demonstrate a bounded system that:
A) reliably converts transient throughput into persistent state
B) while genuinely lacking one of the five phases (not merely hiding it)
STM-5 predicts:

1. Efficiency limits are dominated by Phase 2 and Phase 3 losses (retention/redistribution
+ conversion), not by “capture” alone.
2. Systems that appear “mysteriously efficient” will reveal a strong Phase-2 
coherence/retention mechanism and a clean Phase-3 conversion gate.
3. When persistent-state accumulation fails in real deployments, the failure mode will trace 
to one (or more) of:
o poor coupling (Phase 1)
o uncontrolled leakage/scatter (Phase 2)
o weak conversion / wrong mode (Phase 3)
o lack of rectification/selection (Phase 4)
o no commit/vent path (Phase 5)
These are testable engineering diagnostics.
Notes on scope (keeps it physics-safe)
 STM-5 introduces no new forces and does not compete with established physics.
 It is a systems-level invariance: a grammar for transformation pipelines observed across 
domains (thermodynamics, radiative transport, control systems, information-processing 
systems).
 “Observer” is functional, not metaphysical: it means a mechanism that breaks 
symmetry and selects outcomes (gating, thresholds, feedback).
Optional compact form (for a paper abstract)
STM-5 formalizes a minimum five-phase pipeline required for any bounded system to 
convert transient flux into persistent state: capture → internal redistribution → mode 
conversion → selection/work → resolution (storage or dissipation). The law is constraint￾derived, domain-agnostic, and falsifiable by counterexample.
